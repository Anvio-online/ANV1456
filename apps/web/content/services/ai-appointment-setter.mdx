Most "AI receptionist" products sold to small businesses are a menu tree with a synthetic voice — press one for sales, press two for support. That is not what this is. An AI appointment setter holds an actual conversation: it answers, works out what the caller wants and whether they are a fit, offers real times from your calendar, and books one — then leaves you a note on who called and why.

## What it does on a call

The sequence is always the same, and it is short. It picks up and says, in the first sentence, that it is an AI assistant calling on behalf of your business. It asks the two or three questions that decide whether a caller is worth a slot — location, what they need, rough timing — the ones a person asks every single time. If the caller is a fit, it reads out real openings from your calendar, confirms one, and books it. If they are not, it takes their details and says someone will follow up.

At the end, you get the transcript, the caller's number, and a one-line summary: booked for Thursday 3pm, wants a kitchen quote — or not a fit, wanted something you don't do.

## What it actually replaces

The specific thing it replaces is a person stopping what they are doing to answer a call, ask the same qualifying questions, and negotiate a time against a calendar they have to check. For a business where the phone is the main way work comes in — trades, clinics, salons, service firms — that interruption happens all day, and the calls that come when nobody can pick up are lost outright.

It is not trying to replace the conversation where you actually win the work. It handles the part before that: the triage and the scheduling.

<Callout type="note">
  It discloses that it is an AI on every call, by design. If that feels like it would put your
  callers off, this is probably not the right tool for your business — and that is worth knowing
  before you build it, not after.
</Callout>

## How it connects to your systems

It books into the calendar you already run on. It reads your genuine availability, so it never offers a time that is taken, and a slot it books shows up the same as one you entered by hand. If you use a scheduling tool, it works through that; if you use a shared Google Calendar, it works with that directly. The setup work is mostly in the qualifying questions — deciding what a good caller looks like for you, and what the AI should do with everyone else.

## What it costs and how long it takes

|                                | Typical shape                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Build cost                     | Starting from ₹30,000 for one calendar, one qualifying flow — full scope quoted after a discovery call |
| Timeline to first live version | 2–3 weeks                                                                                              |
| Ongoing cost                   | A small per-minute charge for the voice and phone number while it runs                                 |

The build cost tracks how many kinds of call it has to handle and how many systems it has to touch, not how busy your phone is. A quiet line and a busy one cost about the same to set up.

## What you own afterward

The call flow, the qualifying logic, the calendar connection, and the documentation are yours, handed over. If you bring the work in-house later or move to another provider, nothing here is built to make that hard.

## When an AI appointment setter is the wrong call

If your first phone conversation _is_ the sale — if the person who picks up is closing, not scheduling — putting an AI in front of that costs you more than it saves. If every call genuinely needs a human read on tone and situation, the same. And if your call volume is low enough that voicemail plus a same-day callback already loses you nothing, this is solving a problem you do not have; spend the money elsewhere.

It also does not fix a calendar nobody keeps up to date. If your availability lives in someone's head, step one is writing it down — useful on its own, and required before any of this works.
